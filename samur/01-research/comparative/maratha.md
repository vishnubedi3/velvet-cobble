# Maratha Confederacy (c. 1674–1818) — Comparative Research

Status: RESEARCH
Date: 2026-08-26
Provenance: memory-based (agent training knowledge). Specific figures/dates marked **[verify]** before load-bearing use.
Scope: a confederal, terrain-rooted, merchant-financed military state — a model for a contractual (not institutional) empire that collapses when a third power offers better contracts.

## 1. Material foundation

- **Heartland:** the **Konkan coast + Deccan plateau**, with the **Sahyadri (Western) Ghats** as a defensible, artillery- and cavalry-unfriendly core; conquered zones (Gujarat, Malwa) as the revenue periphery.
- **Agriculture:** dry rice/millet, sugar, indigo; high rural density and a village agrarian base that can be mobilized for guerrilla war.
- **Trade:** Konkan ports (Ratnagiri, Dabhol) exporting cloth to the Southeast Asian trade.
- **Strategic setting:** the Ghats make the core **hard to invade but hard to project from**; the empire's reach (via tribute) exceeds its ability to hold territory.

## 2. Core institutions and mechanisms

1. **Dual structure: the Peshwa bureaucratic center + the semi-autonomous sardars.** The **Peshwa** (prime minister/regent, seated at Satara/Pune) ran the imperial center — collecting revenue, coordinating campaigns, running the bureaucracy. The **sardars / royal houses** (the Ashtadiggaj, "eight gazelles") held hereditary command of regional forces and territories. **Mechanism:** solves the problem of mobilizing a dispersed agrarian-military elite **without hereditary land grants** (the land is too thin for a jagir system) — it uses revenue-sharing and contract instead. **Unintended:** after the Peshwa center weakened (mid-18th c.), the sardars became de facto independent states; the confederacy's cohesion was **contractual, not institutional**, and it collapsed when the British offered better contracts (the Subsidiary Alliances, 1774/1802/1817–18).
2. **Chauth and Sardeshmukhi (tribute without administration).** *Chauth* (~25%, the "fourth") and *sardeshmukhi* (~2.5%, the "headship") levied on territory **under Maratha protection rather than direct rule**. **Mechanism:** a fiscal model that extracts revenue **without paying for governance** — a protection-and-tribute system that converts defeated neighbors into revenue sources. **Unintended:** it creates a class of Maratha revenue agents (the system the British later inherited and regularized); it defines the empire's *de facto* boundary by the reach of the levy, which **outstrips the administrative capacity to hold territory** — the gap between tribute-reach and conquest.
3. **Merchant-banked war (the shahukars).** The Bania merchant-banker class financed campaigns with loans against **future chauth**; war was a commercial enterprise (capturing revenue = repaying the loan). **Mechanism:** separates war finance from land revenue, enabling campaigns beyond the home base. **Unintended:** war becomes **debt-financed**, so campaigns *had to succeed* or the banking class defaulted — the Peshwa state had a **structural growth imperative** (it needed continuous conquest/tribute to service the debt).
4. **Military system.** Light cavalry (*shiledar*, levied from agrarian-military castes) plus the *gupta/dash* (irregulars for raiding, foraging, and harassing supply lines); professionalized under the Peshwas (artillery, fort siege). Logistics by mule; campaigns bound to the seasons (the monsoon). **Unintended:** the *gupta* system's brutality creates cycles of reprisal; the irregulars **cannot be stood down** after a defeat (a known 18th-c. problem).
5. **Adoption and legitimization.** Shahu's adoption of a Peshwa as regent (1713) let the **bureaucratic center overtake the hereditary royal house**; the later royal houses resented the Peshwa primacy. **Mechanism:** adoption is a tool to install a bureaucratic center over a dynastic one.

## 3. Elite structure and factions

- The Peshwa center (the bureaucratic-military elite); the sardars/royal houses (the hereditary regional commanders); the shahukar banking class (the war financiers); the agrarian-military castes (the cavalry base); the Varkari-bhakti religious culture (the legitimizing narrative — Shivaji as protector of dharma). The recurring fault line is **Peshwa centralization vs. sardar autonomy**, and the **bankers' growth imperative** pulling the state toward war.

## 4. Expansion, contraction, and decline

- **1660s–1707:** Shivaji's expansion into the Mughal periphery; after the Mughal succession war (1707) the Marathas dominate the north.
- **1730s–1761:** the Peshwas push north; **the Third Battle of Panipat (1761)** is a disaster. **Mechanism:** over-extension of a northward campaign + supply-line failure + internal disunity + a concentrated Afghan (Durrani) army. **Effect:** northward expansion stops; sardar fragmentation accelerates; over the following decades the confederacy becomes a set of **treaty partners of the British** until 1818.

## 5. Mechanisms available for Samur transformation

- **Tribute-without-administration as a fiscal model** (chauth) and its limit (the tribute-reach exceeds the holding-capacity).
- **Merchant-banked war** and its **growth imperative** (debt-financed war must keep conquering).
- **A defensible terrain core** (the Ghats) as the empire's heartland — hard to invade, hard to project from.
- **A contractual confederacy vs. an institutional state**, and the collapse mode when a third power offers better contracts.
- **Adoption as a tool** to install a bureaucratic center over a dynastic one.
- **A religious narrative as mobilizing identity without a powerful clerical class** (bhakti/Varkari).
- **The irregular (gupta) system** — its tactical value and its inability to stand down.
