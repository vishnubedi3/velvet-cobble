---

The water came up through the old channel before the priests did.

Thesra heard it first — the sound the spring made in the hour before the sun cleared the eastern ridge, a low continuous voice that had been speaking since before the temple had a name for it. She stood at the garden's edge where the cut-stone walkway gave way to packed earth and watched the channel's mouth darken with the day's first flow. The water was cold. It was always cold. The priests said the spring drew from beneath the mountain's root, where the stone was older than the wind, and Thesra believed them the way she believed the channels themselves — not as doctrine but as something she had never had reason to test.

She had been born in the rooms above the eastern cloister, in the year the Besra Head Temple's third conciliar appointee died of a fever no one could name. She knew this because the temple kept records of everything, including the births of people whose existence was itself a kind of record. She was the House of Kesra. The temple was her house. These two facts were the same fact, stated in two registers.

The channel filled. The water ran clear over the cut stone and darkened where it touched the older masonry — the rough blocks the Dharan builders had set around the spring's original mouth, eight hundred years of mineral staining marking the line where the new work met the old. Thesra crouched and put her hand in the flow. Cold. The same cold. Every morning of her life.

Beyond the garden, the temple was waking. She could hear the junior priests moving through the lower halls, the soft percussion of bare feet on stone, the murmur of the morning recitations beginning in the small practice rooms where the Shastras students learned their first commentaries. The sound carried differently in the wet season. The air was heavier now, month three of the sea-wind, and the temple's stone held the night's rain the way a bowl holds water — everything damp, everything conducting sound along the walls and through the courtyards. In the dry months the temple was quieter, drier, more contained. Now it breathed.

She stood and followed the channel back toward the inner court. The path took her past the old wall — the low retaining wall at the garden's western edge, where the mountain's natural rock showed through the temple's foundations. The old carvings were here. Not the Dharan-era reliefs that covered the Great Temple's outer walls — those were formal, ordered, the consecration line rendered in the frozen orthography with the careful proportions of the high imperial style. These were older. Cut into the living rock with tools that had left rougher marks, in a script so archaic that only the senior scholars could read more than a word or two. The old stone, the priests called it. The old stone speaks the old dharma.

Thesra could not read it. She could read the frozen orthography — the sacred script of the Charter, every letter precise, every stroke carrying the weight of the re-fixing that had held it unchanged for fourteen centuries. She could read the Orenic hand that the administrators used for the temple's accounts and correspondence. But the old stone's marks were older than both, older than the re-fixing, older than the Charter itself. The Veshna stratum, the scholars called it, when they thought she was not listening.

She passed the old wall every morning. She had never asked what the carvings said. The question had always seemed to her like asking what the spring said — as if the water's voice were a language you could translate, rather than something that preceded language entirely.

The inner court opened before her. The Great Temple's central space — the Dharan builders' work, the high vaulted hall where the guru's seat stood and where the consecration rite had been performed for six hundred and seventy-five years. Morning light came through the eastern clerestory and fell in long bars across the stone floor. The guru's seat was a raised stone platform at the hall's western end, carved with the investiture symbols, worn smooth by the knees of every guru since the founding.

And beside it, two paces to the right, the other seat.

The protector's seat. The throne. Empty, as it had been empty for two hundred and thirty-seven years.

Thesra did not look at it directly. She had been raised not to. The temple's etiquette around the empty seat was precise and unspoken: you did not stare, you did not ignore it, you did not sit near it, you did not speak of it to visitors. You acknowledged it the way you acknowledged the spring — as something that was there, that had always been there, that would be there when you were gone.

A junior priest was sweeping the hall's floor with a palm-fibre broom. He looked up when Thesra entered, saw her, and lowered his eyes. This too was the etiquette. She was the Temple Line. The deposed House of Kesra's blood ran in her, the consecrated line that had held the protector's seat from the founding to the deposition. She was sacred. She was untouchable. She was twenty-eight years old and had never been further than the temple's outer wall.

"Has the guru risen?" she asked.

"The guru is in the reading room, Thesra-bai." The junior priest's voice was careful, respectful, the voice of a man speaking to someone who was technically his superior in the temple's ritual hierarchy and practically a prisoner in its political one. "He asked that you join him before the midday meal."

She nodded and turned back toward the eastern cloister. The wet stone was cool under her feet. Somewhere in the lower temple, the Shastras students were chanting the fourteenth commentary — she could hear the rising cadence of the dharma-by-station passage, the lines every student learned before they were allowed to handle the Charter itself.

*The king protects. The priest consecrates. The warrior defends. The merchant trades honestly. The peasant cultivates and renders the tribute. Each in their station; each by their karma; each toward the fulfilment of the dharma and the good death.*

She had heard these lines every morning since she was old enough to hear anything. They were the grammar of the world. She knew them the way she knew the spring's voice — not as information but as the condition of being alive.

The reading room was in the eastern wing, where the library held the Shastras cycle — the sixty-odd commentaries that the matha's scholars had produced across the centuries, interpreting and reinterpreting the Charter's short foundational text. The guru sat at a low table near the room's single window, a palm-leaf manuscript open before him. He was an old man. He had been old when Thesra was born. His name was not something she used; in the temple, he was the guru, the way the spring was the spring.

"Thesra-bai." He did not look up from the manuscript. "Sit."

She sat on the mat opposite him. The room smelled of palm oil and the faint mustiness that the wet season brought to every enclosed space in the temple. Through the window she could see the outer courtyard, where the temple's administrative staff were already at work — the accountants with their tallies, the grain inspectors who came each morning from the devanama lands to report the season's progress.

"The Sabha meets in seven days," the guru said.

"Yes."

"The Besra seat is vacant. The rump's Chiefs' Council has proposed a candidate. The Tarn wing has objected. The Threna head and the Keshkhor head are undecided." He turned a page of the manuscript with a care that suggested the leaf was fragile. "The Voren head will abstain, as the Phre always abstains on internal appointments. The Kesveth head will follow the Vethra's interest, which is to maintain the Concordat's terms and avoid setting a precedent that would allow the rump to appoint heads unilaterally."

Thesra said nothing. This was not a conversation that required her response. The guru was informing her, not consulting her.

"The Tarn wing's objection is not to the candidate's qualifications," he continued. "The candidate is competent. The objection is to the principle — that the rump should not have the power to fill a Sabha seat without the conciliar process. The Tarn wing's position is that the Sabha must appoint, not the rump."

"The Tarn wing's position," Thesra said, "is that the Tarn wing has been losing arguments for thirty years and has decided to start winning them on procedure."

The guru looked at her then. His eyes were clear. He had been the Veshna apex for forty years; he had presided over the first Sabha, the Consecration Moratorium, the five Concordats, the Sareth Demand, the Phre Silver crisis. He had held the matha together through the conciliar system and the pragmatist faction's compromises, and he had done it by being, above all things, patient.

"The Tarn wing has a point," he said.

"The Tarn wing always has a point. That is what makes it dangerous."

He closed the manuscript. "I need you to be present at the Sabha meeting."

The request was unusual. The Temple Line did not attend Sabha sessions. The Line's existence was a political fact — the deposed House of Kesra, sheltered at the Great Temple, the living proof of the consecrated line's survival — but it was not a political actor. The Line did not vote, did not speak, did not participate in the matha's governance. It existed. It was sheltered. It was sacred. That was its function.

"Why?" she asked.

"Because the Besra seat is in the rump. Because the candidate is the rump's choice. Because if the Sabha confirms the rump's appointment, the Tarn wing will claim the process was illegitimate — that the rump used the Temple Line's presence to sanctify a partisan decision." He paused. "And if the Sabha rejects the rump's appointment, the Chiefs' Council will claim the Sabha is overreaching — that the conciliar body is usurping the rump's internal governance."

"You want me there so that neither side can claim the other used me."

"I want you there so that both sides must acknowledge your presence without being able to use it."

Thesra looked at her hands. They were clean, well-kept, the hands of a woman who had never worked the land or handled a weapon or touched anything rougher than palm-leaf manuscript and temple stone. The hands of the Temple Line. The hands that had poured the ritual water at every major temple ceremony since she was old enough to hold the vessel.

"I am a prop," she said.

"You are a symbol. There is a difference."

"A symbol has meaning. A prop has a position."

The guru was silent for a moment. Then he said: "You will pour the water for the Sabha delegates. You will not speak. You will not respond to questions. You will be seen, and you will be gone. That is all I ask."

"And if the Tarn delegation speaks to me?"

"They will not. The envoy is Kheshan of the Keshath household. He knows the protocol."

Thesra stood. "Then I will pour the water."

---

The library occupied the temple's southern wing — two long rooms with high ceilings and narrow windows, the palm-leaf manuscripts stored in wooden racks that ran the length of the walls. The Shastras cycle occupied the first room: the sixty-three commentaries, arranged chronologically, from the earliest (the first guru's notes on the Charter, KE 40–55) to the most recent (the conciliar commentaries, produced after the first Sabha, post-KE 700). The second room held the administrative records — the tax tallies, the land grants, the correspondence with the seven Head Temples, the Concordat texts.

Vesharan was in the second room, cross-referencing the Voren Concordat against the port-law provisions in the Beshara Code's surviving fragments. He was twenty-four, three years out of the Shastras training, assigned to the library because he had a good hand and a better memory and because the guru had decided, some months ago, that Vesharan needed the kind of patience that only came from handling old documents.

He looked up when Thesra entered. He had grown up in the temple — his father was a mid-level administrator in the devanama office, his mother a weaver in the temple's cloth workshop — and he had known Thesra since they were children, in the way that children in a small enclosed community know each other: by face, by habit, by the particular way each person moved through the spaces they shared. But they were not friends. The Temple Line did not have friends, in the way that the throne did not have cushions. There were protocols.

"Thesra-bai." He stood, the formal half-bow.

"Vesharan." She moved to the rack where the pre-fragmentation correspondence was stored — the letters between the Great Temple and the Head Temples during the last decades of the unified empire. She was not supposed to read these. She was not forbidden to read them either. The distinction was one of those fine lines the temple drew around her, like the lines the old builders had drawn around the spring — not walls, exactly, but channels.

"What are you looking for?" he asked.

"The last letter from Tarenam before the division. I want to see what the Tarn wing was saying before the succession crisis became public."

Vesharan considered this. "That correspondence is restricted. The guru's office controls access to the pre-division Tarn materials."

"I know."

"Then you know I cannot give them to you."

"I know that too." She pulled a manuscript from an adjacent rack — a collection of festival rites from the early High Empire, harmless, uncontested. "But you could tell me, in conversation, whether the last Tarenam letter was written before or after the Kesra III claim."

He looked at her. She was standing in the library's narrow light, the manuscript in her hands, her face unreadable. He knew what she was doing. She was using the one form of access the temple's protocols allowed her — the casual conversation between two people who had known each other all their lives — to obtain information she could not request through official channels.

"The letter was written three months before the Kesra III claim became public," he said. "It was written in the Tarn wing's hand — the Tarn vernacular, not the frozen orthography. It was addressed to the guru directly, not through the Sabha."

"And the content?"

"I cannot tell you the content."

"But you can tell me the tone."

He paused. "The tone was not conciliatory."

Thesra set the festival manuscript back on the rack. "Thank you, Vesharan."

"Thesra-bai." He hesitated. "The Tarn delegation arrives tomorrow. The envoy is Kheshan of the Keshath household."

"I know. The guru told me."

"He is not what you might expect."

"What might I expect?"

Vesharan did not answer directly. He said: "The Tarn wing's position has changed since the Claim Moratorium. They are not pressing the Keshath claim this time. They are pressing the procedure."

"Which is more dangerous."

"Yes."

She left the library without another word. Vesharan watched her go — the straight back, the measured step, the particular way she held herself that was neither pride nor submission but something he had never found a name for. He returned to the Voren Concordat and tried to concentrate on the port-law provisions. The words swam on the palm leaf. He had the sense, which he could not quite articulate, that something was shifting in the temple's careful equilibrium — that the Sabha meeting in seven days was not the routine procedural matter the guru's public statements made it seem.

---

The Tarn delegation arrived in the afternoon of the following day, by boat on the Oren.

Thesra watched from the upper gallery of the eastern cloister, where the Temple Line's rooms opened onto a narrow balcony that overlooked the temple's river landing. The Oren was high — month three of a strong-wind year, the river running brown with silt from the upper watershed, the current strong enough that the delegation's boat had to be poled against the flow for the last hundred yards to the landing.

Three boats. The lead boat carried the envoy and his personal staff. The second carried the delegation's administrative officers — the scribes, the accountants, the men who would spend the next week examining the temple's records and verifying the candidate's qualifications. The third carried the guard — twelve men in the Tarn Confederation's travelling dress, which was not the formal military uniform but was not civilian clothing either. The Tarn had been dressing their diplomatic missions this way since the Sareth Demand, when the Threnath house had started sending its envoys in full garrison dress and the other polities had been forced to decide whether to match them.

The envoy disembarked first. He was younger than Thesra had expected — perhaps her own age, perhaps a few years older, with the lean build of a man who spent time on horseback and the easy balance of someone accustomed to boats. He wore a plain travelling cloak over a tunic of undyed cotton, and the only mark of his office was the small bronze brooch at his shoulder — the Keshath house's device, which she recognized from the correspondence she was not supposed to have read.

Kheshan of the Keshath household. The name itself was a statement. Keshath was the Tarn's leading house — the founding-marriage descendant, the house that claimed the throne by the dynastic precedent of Kesra I's marriage to a chief's daughter. The envoy's given name, Kheshan, was ordinary enough — a common Samur name, from the consonant-rich register. But the house name was the one that carried weight. He had not come as a neutral Tarn functionary. He had come as the Keshath.

Vesharan was at the landing to receive the delegation, along with two senior priests and the temple's chief administrator. The greetings were formal, conducted in the standard Samur of the upper Oren — the court dialect, the administrative register. Thesra could hear fragments of the exchange from the gallery: the formulaic welcomes, the enquiries about the journey, the ritual offering of water from the spring. The envoy's voice carried well — a clear baritone, unhurried, the accent of the Tarn plain softened by what sounded like years spent in the core.

She watched the delegation process up the landing steps and into the temple's outer courtyard. The envoy paused at the threshold of the inner gate — the gate that separated the public temple from the restricted inner courts — and looked up at the Great Temple's facade. For a moment, his expression shifted. Not awe, exactly. Something more complicated. Recognition, perhaps. The look of a man seeing something he had read about many times and was encountering for the first time in its physical reality.

Then the expression was gone, and he was through the gate, and the delegation was inside.

---

The Sabha met in the conciliar hall — a rectangular room in the temple's western wing, built after the first Veshna Sabha (post-KE 700) when it became clear that the old imperial council chamber was too large and too associated with the House of Kesra's rule. The new room was smaller, plainer, designed for eight seats around a central table. The walls were bare except for a single inscription in the frozen orthography — the Charter's opening passage, the dharma grammar in its purest form.

Thesra arrived an hour before the session began. The hall was empty. The eight seats were arranged around the table: the guru's seat at the western end, the seven Head Temple heads' seats along the sides. Each seat had a small wooden marker bearing the temple's name in the Orenic hand. Kesra. Besra. Voren. Threna. Keshkhor. Kesveth. Tarenam.

The Besra seat was empty. Its marker lay face-down on the table — the sign of a vacancy. The head of the Besra Head Temple had died two months ago, and the seat had been empty since. This was what the Sabha was about: filling the vacancy, resolving the dispute over who had the right to appoint, and — beneath the surface — testing the balance of power between the rump and the Tarn wing.

She found the water vessel where it was always kept — a bronze pitcher, old, the Dharan-era work, with the investiture symbols engraved on its sides. She filled it from the channel that ran through the conciliar hall's floor — a branch of the spring's main channel, the same cold water that fed the garden and the inner court and the old wall. She set the pitcher on the side table, with the eight cups arranged in the prescribed order.

Then she stood in the corner and waited.

The delegates arrived in ones and twos. The Kesra head first — an old woman, the guru's ally, who nodded to Thesra without speaking. The Keshkhor head next — a broad-shouldered man with the Khor frontier's weathered skin, who had been appointed during the Phre Silver crisis and had spent his tenure managing the rump-Khor silver axis. The Threna head — the Sareth Dhaneshra's representative, quiet, careful, the man who balanced every statement between the rump's interest and the Threnath house's pride.

The Tarenam head came in with the envoy. The Tarn wing's representative was a tall woman in her fifties, dressed in the Tarn renewal style — the simple white cotton of the Tarnesh Dawn's devotional tradition, without the formal vestments the other heads wore. She was the Tarn wing's voice in the Sabha, and she had been pressing the conciliar position for fifteen years.

Kheshan stood behind her, at the room's edge. He was not a delegate. He was the envoy — the Tarn Confederation's representative to the matha, carrying the Keshath house's interest without having a vote. His eyes moved around the room, taking in the arrangement of seats, the inscription on the wall, the empty Besra marker.

Then they found Thesra in her corner.

He looked at her for a moment — not the way the junior priests looked at her, with averted eyes, and not the way the guru looked at her, with the careful assessment of a man managing a political instrument. He looked at her the way one looks at a person one has heard about but never met. With curiosity. With something that might have been sympathy.

She looked back.

The Voren head arrived last — the Phre zone's representative, a man of indeterminate age with the careful neutrality of someone who served a foreign power's enclave and survived by not offending anyone. He took his seat without looking at Thesra.

The guru entered. The room stood. He took his seat, and the others sat, and the session began.

Thesra poured the water. She moved from seat to seat in the prescribed order — the guru first, then clockwise around the table, each cup filled from the bronze pitcher, each cup received with a small nod of acknowledgment. When she reached the Tarenam head, the woman's fingers brushed Thesra's as she took the cup. The touch was deliberate. A signal, perhaps. Or perhaps just a woman touching another woman's hand in a room full of men.

When she reached Kheshan, standing at the room's edge, she hesitated. The protocol did not account for the envoy. He was not a delegate. He was not seated. But he was present, and he was thirsty, and the water was from the spring.

She filled a ninth cup and held it out to him.

He took it. "Thank you," he said, in the standard Samur. Then, more quietly, in the Tarn variant — the softer vowels, the different cadence: "You are the Temple Line."

It was not a question.

"I am Thesra," she said.

"I know." He drank. "In Tarenam, they say the spring has been speaking since before the first carving. That the old stone's marks are not words but the sound the water made, written down."

"The old stone is older than the temple."

"Everything is older than the temple." He set the cup on the side table. "That is what makes the temple necessary."

Before she could respond, the guru's voice rose — the opening statement of the session, the formal framing of the Besra vacancy. Thesra returned to her corner. The session began.

---

The debate lasted three hours.

Thesra listened. She had been raised in the temple; she understood the language of conciliar politics the way a sailor understands weather — not as an abstraction but as the medium in which she lived. The rump's candidate was a competent administrator, a man named Bhadren who had served the Kesra Head Temple for twenty years and had the support of the Chiefs' Council. The Tarn wing's objection was procedural: the rump had proposed the candidate without submitting the name to the Sabha for approval, which the Tarn wing argued violated the Concordat's terms.

The rump's position, presented by the Kesra head, was that the Besra Head Temple was in the rump's territory, that the Concordat gave the rump the right to propose candidates for Head Temples within its jurisdiction, and that the Sabha's role was to confirm or reject — not to nominate.

The Tarn wing's position, presented by the Tarenam head, was that the Concordat gave the rump the right to propose only with the Sabha's prior consultation, that the consultation had not occurred, and that the process was therefore invalid regardless of the candidate's qualifications.

The Threna head asked whether the candidate had any connection to the Gheshar house. The Kesra head said no. The Keshkhor head asked whether the Tarn wing had an alternative candidate. The Tarenam head said the Tarn wing was not proposing a candidate; it was objecting to the process. The Voren head abstained, as expected. The Kesveth head said the Vethra's interest was in the precedent — that no polity should have the unilateral right to appoint a Head Temple head, because the next such appointment might affect the Kesveth Concordat's terms.

Kheshan stood at the room's edge and said nothing. He was the envoy. He was not a delegate. His role was to observe, to carry the Keshath house's interest into the room without expressing it, and to report back to Tarenam.

The guru listened. He asked two questions — both procedural, both designed to clarify the Concordat's text rather than to advance any faction's position. Then he proposed a compromise: the rump would withdraw the current nomination, submit the candidate's name to the Sabha for formal consultation, and the Sabha would reconvene in thirty days to confirm or reject. The compromise preserved the rump's right to propose while establishing the conciliar consultation as a binding step.

The Tarenam head accepted. The Kesra head accepted, with visible reluctance. The other heads assented. The session ended.

Thesra collected the cups. The bronze pitcher was empty. The delegates filed out, the Tarenam head pausing at the door to speak quietly with the guru, the Threna head already in conversation with the Keshkhor head about something that was not the Besra vacancy.

Kheshan was the last to leave. He stopped at the side table where Thesra was gathering the cups.

"You poured a ninth cup," he said.

"You were thirsty."

"The protocol does not account for the envoy."

"No."

He looked at her again — that same look, the curiosity without pity. "In the Tarn, we have a saying about the Empty Throne. 'The seat waits for the one who does not want it.'"

"That is not a saying here."

"No. Here, you do not speak of it at all." He moved toward the door. "That is the difference between the Tarn and the rump. We say the things you are not allowed to say."

He left. Thesra stood alone in the conciliar hall, the empty cups on the table, the Besra marker still face-down, the inscription on the wall casting its long shadows in the afternoon light.

*The king protects. The priest consecrates. The warrior defends. The merchant trades honestly. The peasant cultivates and renders the tribute.*

There was no king. The protector's seat in the inner court was empty. The grammar had a subject with no one to fill it.

She picked up the bronze pitcher and carried it back to the channel, where the spring's cold water ran through the temple's floor as it had run before the temple was built, before the Dharan builders cut the channels, before the old stone's marks were carved into the living rock. She filled the pitcher and stood for a moment, listening.

The spring's voice was the same voice. It had been speaking since before the first carving, and it would speak after the last council had met and the last compromise had been recorded and the lastEmpty Throne had been filled or left empty forever.

She poured the water back into the channel and watched it rejoin the flow.

---

That evening, Vesharan found her in the garden, sitting on the low wall beside the old carvings.

The sun had dropped behind the western ridge. The garden was in shadow. The channel's water was audible but not visible — a voice in the dark, continuous, indifferent to the temple's politics.

"The session went as the guru expected," Vesharan said, standing at a respectful distance.

"The compromise was already decided before the delegation arrived."

"Yes."

"Then why was I there?"

Vesharan considered this. "Because the compromise required a witness. Because the Tarn wing needed to see you and know that the rump was not using the Temple Line to sanctify the process. Because the rump needed to see you and know that the Tarn wing was not ignoring your existence."

"I was a witness to my own irrelevance."

"You were a witness to the fact that your existence is the one thing both sides agree on."

Thesra was quiet. Then she said: "Kheshan spoke to me."

"The envoy?"

"He told me a saying from the Tarn. About the Empty Throne."

Vesharan waited.

"He said: 'The seat waits for the one who does not want it.'"

They stood in the dark garden, the spring's voice between them. The old carvings were invisible now — just the rough texture of the living rock under Thesra's fingers, the marks of people who had stood here before the temple, before the Charter, before the founding, and had cut their voices into the stone because the stone was the only thing that would hold them.

"Vesharan."

"Yes."

"What does the old stone say?"

He was quiet for a long time. Then: "I do not know. No one alive knows. The scholars can read a word here, a word there. The full text is gone. The re-fixing preserved the forms but not the content. The old stone's marks are the oldest writing in the world, and we cannot read them."

"Then what are they?"

"They are proof that someone was here before us. That someone stood at this spring and thought something worth writing down. That the thought survived even though its meaning did not."

Thesra ran her fingers along the carving's edge. The stone was cold. The channel's water ran beneath the wall, and above them the first stars were appearing in the gap between the ridge and the sky — the same stars, she thought, that had appeared above this spring every night for longer than anyone could count, watched by people whose names were gone, whose languages were gone, whose cities were the buried mounds the farmers ploughed around without knowing what they were.

"The seat waits for the one who does not want it," she said.

"Perhaps," Vesharan said. "Or perhaps it waits for no one. Perhaps the waiting is the seat's own condition, and the one who fills it — if anyone ever does — will find that the seat was never waiting at all."

She looked at him. In the dark, his face was just a shape, a suggestion of features. She had known him all her life and did not know him. The temple held them both and kept them apart, the way it held the spring and kept its water moving through the channels without ever letting it pool.

"Go to bed, Vesharan."

"Yes, Thesra-bai."

He left. She stayed. The spring spoke. The old stone held its unreadable marks. The Empty Throne sat in the dark inner court, and the protector's seat waited for the one who did not want it, or for no one, or for something that had not yet been imagined.

The wind shifted. The sea-wind brought the smell of rain from the south, from the delta, from the Oren's mouth where the Phre held the chokepoint and the silver moved through the enclaves and the world's trade passed through a strait no wider than the temple's main channel. Somewhere to the east, the Tarn delegation's boats were tied at the landing, and Kheshan of the Keshath household was writing his first report to Tarenam. Somewhere to the west, the Sareth's iron mines were dark and quiet, and the Threnath house was counting its silver. Somewhere to the north, the Khor clans were moving with their horses toward the summer pasture, and the bad-grass year was still two seasons away.

And in the garden, the spring spoke, and Thesra listened, and the night passed, and the temple held.
