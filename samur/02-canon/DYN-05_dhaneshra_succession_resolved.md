## 1. The question restated (Q-080)

The Dhaneshra Period (post-KE ~900) is defined by the **Empty Throne** (the Consecration Moratorium — the matha will not consecrate a new king while the four claims are contested) and the **four competing claims** to the protector-of-dharma title (the king's title, the object of the consecration rite, REL-02 §3):

| The claim | The claimant | The basis |
|---|---|---|
| **The true-line claim** | The **Temple Line** (the deposed House of Kesra, sheltered at the Great Temple) | The consecrated royal house — the line that held the throne before the deposition (KE 675). |
| **The founding-marriage claim** | The **Keshath house** (the Tarn Confederation's leading house) | The dynastic-by-marriage precedent — Kesra I's marriage to a chief's daughter whose descendants founded the Keshath house (DYN-03 §2 #1). |
| **The office claim** | The **Gheshar house** (the Kesra Dhaneshra, the rump) | The founding Shreshtha's line — the office that predates the dynasty (Gheshar I, DYN-02 §3); the dynasty is gone; the office survives. |
| **The defender claim** | The **Threnath house** (the Sareth Dhaneshra) | The wall — the Sareth's passes, forests, and iron; the garrison commander's line (DYN-03 §1); the protector of the western approaches. |

The question is not "who will win in a war?" — it is "which claim is the matha most likely to consecrate, given the institutional logic of the consecration rite, the matha's own interests, and the power distribution among the polities?" The answer is determined by the established canonical structure, not by narrative convenience.

## 2. The matha's consecration logic (the rite's requirements)

**What does the matha need to consecrate a king?** The consecration rite (REL-02 §3) requires:
1. **A legitimate claimant** (the matha must judge the claimant's right to rule — the line, the marriage precedent, the office, or the service).
2. **A stable territorial base** (the king must control enough of the Oren heartland to sustain the grain tribute that funds the temple economy — the matha's tax-exempt temple lands, ~10–15% of the arable, depend on the king's fiscal order, REL-02 §4).
3. **A military capacity to protect dharma** (the king is the protector-of-dharma; the matha will not consecrate a king who cannot hold the position — the deposition of the House of Kesra is the canonical example of what happens when a king loses military control, TIM-03 §7).
4. **The matha's own institutional interest** (the matha will not consecrate a king who will diminish the matha's autonomy — the matha's deterrence function [REL-02 §8] and its own survival interest [the two-wing + concordat system] mean the matha will not consecrate a king who threatens the Concordats or the matha's tax-exempt lands).

**The Consecration Moratorium's logic:** The matha imposed the Moratorium (post-KE ~700) because no single claimant met all four requirements simultaneously. The Temple Line had legitimacy (#1) but no military or territorial base (the rump's chiefs deposed it; it survives only under matha shelter). The Gheshar house had the office (#3) and the rump's base but lacked the true-line legitimacy (#1 — the dynasty is gone; the office is not the line). The Keshath house had the marriage precedent (#1) and the Tarn's base but lacked the Oren heartland (#2 — the Tarn holds the crossings, not the core). The Threnath house had the defender role (#3) and the Sareth's resources but lacked the Oren heartland (#2) and the legitimacy (#1 — the Threnath is a military house, not a royal one).

**The critical insight:** The Moratorium is not permanent. It is a **political equilibrium** — it holds as long as the power distribution remains balanced. The moment one claimant gains enough of all four requirements simultaneously, the matha faces the choice: consecrate, or watch the claimant gain power without matha sanction and diminish the matha's authority. The matha's institutional interest is to consecrate **before** the claimant becomes strong enough to不需要 it — a pattern established by the founding itself (Kesra I was consecrated at the moment of his power's peak, but before he could rule without the matha, REL-02 §3).

## 3. The four claims assessed against the consecration requirements

### 3.1 The Temple Line (the true-line claim)

- **Legitimacy (requirement #1):** The strongest of the four. The Temple Line is the consecrated royal house — the line that held the throne for 675 years (KE 0–675). The matha's own records (the rite-records, REL-02 §7 — the third source tradition) preserve the consecration lineage. The Temple Line's claim is not disputed on grounds of legitimacy; it is disputed on grounds of capacity.
- **Territorial base (requirement #2):** The weakest of the four. The Temple Line has no territorial base. It lives at the Great Temple, sheltered by the matha. The rump's chiefs deposed the line (KE 675) precisely because it had lost military control. The Temple Line cannot fund the temple economy from its own resources — it survives on matha charity and rump tolerance.
- **Military capacity (requirement #3):** None. The Temple Line has no army, no revenue, no administrative structure. The matha's deterrence function (REL-02 §8 — the matha's authority deters the chiefs from killing the consecrated line) protects the Temple Line from murder, but it does not give the line military power.
- **Matha's institutional interest (requirement #4):** Complicated. The matha shelters the Temple Line (§5) — this is both the matha's sacred obligation and its most potent political leverage (the true line is the rump's legitimacy asset; the matha controls access to it). Consecrating the Temple Line would restore the protector-of-dharma order, but it would also elevate the Temple Line to a position of power — and a Temple Line king with military power would be the matha's ally today and its master tomorrow (the historical pattern: the pre-founding matha → the proto-consecration → the imperial matha → the Beshara tension, REL-02 §1/§3/§8). The matha's institutional interest is to **keep the Temple Line sacred but powerless** — the Temple Line as the symbol of legitimacy, not as a ruling house.

**Assessment:** The Temple Line has the strongest legitimacy and the weakest capacity. The matha will not consecrate the Temple Line unless the line somehow acquires a territorial base and military capacity — which requires allying with a polity. The Temple Line's most likely path to the throne runs through the **Gheshar house** (the rump) — a Gheshar-Temple Line union would combine the office claim + the true-line legitimacy, meeting requirements #1 and #3 simultaneously. This is the **Gheshar-Temple Line resolution** (§6.3).

### 3.2 The Keshath house (the founding-marriage claim)

- **Legitimacy (requirement #1):** The marriage precedent is real but secondary. Kesra I's marriage to a chief's daughter established the dynastic-by-marriage principle — but 675 years of consecrated-line rule has privileged the bloodline over the marriage. The matha recognizes the Keshath claim as legitimate but inferior to the Temple Line's. The Second Consecration Question (KE ~850–900) affirmed this: the Keshath claim was recorded but not recognized (DYN-04 §14).
- **Territorial base (requirement #2):** Strong. The Tarn Confederation holds the paddy plain (the highest-yield land in the basin, GEO-01 §4), the Oren crossings, and Tarenam (the Tarn wing's seat). The Tarn's internal arrangement (the Tarnesh fourth, ADM-02 §3) gives the Keshath house fiscal control of the Tarn's wealth.
- **Military capacity (requirement #3):** Moderate. The Tarn's paddy wealth funds a substantial army (bounded by the MIL-02 §1 0.3% ceiling, but the Tarn's high yield means a large absolute army). The Tarn's geography (the water-crossed paddy plain, GEO-01 §4) is defensible — hard to invade, easy to defend. However, the Tarn is landlocked between the rump and the Sareth, with no sea access and no horse supply (the Khor horses go through the rump's Khor frontier, GEO-01 §5).
- **Matha's institutional interest (requirement #4):** The Tarn wing is the matha's internal rival (the Tarnesh Dawn's heirs, REL-02 §5 — the renewal wing vs. the Veshna apex wing). The Tarn wing supports the Keshath claim. Consecrating the Keshath would elevate the Tarn wing's status within the matha — and the Tarn wing's agenda (the Tarn vernacular, the Tarnesh Dawn's renewal) is in tension with the Veshna apex's authority. The matha's apex-neutrality faction (DYN-04 §10) would resist a Keshath consecration precisely because it would strengthen the Tarn wing.

**Assessment:** The Keshath house has the strongest territorial base and a legitimate but secondary claim. The matha's internal politics (the apex-wing vs. the Tarn wing) make the Keshath claim the **matha's least preferred** of the four. The Tarn wing would support it; the Veshna apex wing would oppose it; the matha would split. The Keshath claim can be militarily viable but **cannot be consecrated without fracturing the matha** — unless the matha's internal balance shifts (§6.2).

### 3.3 The Gheshar house (the office claim)

- **Legitimacy (requirement #1):** The office claim is real but partial. The Gheshar house descends from Gheshar I (the founding Shreshtha, DYN-02 §3) — the office predates the dynasty, and the dynasty is gone. But the founding Shreshtha was the king's minister, not the king. The Gheshar house rules the rump through the **Chiefs' Council** (DYN-04 §13) — a post-dynastic government of chiefs, not a royal house. The Gheshar house's claim is that the office of Shreshtha has absorbed the kingly function — a claim the matha has not endorsed.
- **Territorial base (requirement #2):** Moderate. The rump holds the bār core (the fiscal heartland, GEO-01 §4), the Oren canal, Besra (the grain market), Keshkhor (the horse trade), and the Great Temple (the apex). The rump is the weakest of the Veshna polities militarily (DYN-04 §4 — depends on the Sareth's iron/timber and the Khor's horses), but it holds the **legitimacy asset** (the Great Temple + the Temple Line). The rump's base is defensible (the bār is the basin's interior, hard to invade from any direction), but it cannot project power into the Tarn or the Sareth without the Sareth's iron and the Khor's horses.
- **Military capacity (requirement #3):** The weakest of the three viable claimants. The rump's army is bounded by the 0.3% ceiling (MIL-02 §1), and the rump's fiscal base (the bār grain, ECO-02 §1) is sufficient but not exceptional. The rump's strategic vulnerability is the **Khor dependency** (the horses, GEO-01 §5) and the **Sareth dependency** (the iron/timber). If the Khor or the Sareth shifts alignment, the rump's military capacity collapses.
- **Matha's institutional interest (requirement #4):** The strongest alignment of any claimant. The Gheshar house shelters the Temple Line at the Great Temple (§5 — the matha's sacred obligation). The rump holds the Veshna apex wing's seat (the Great Temple, the guru's seat). The matha's apex-neutrality faction supports the Gheshar claim in preference to the Keshath or Threnath — because the Gheshar house's consecration would **not** elevate an internal matha rival (unlike the Keshath-Tarn wing) and would **restore the protector-of-dharma order under the Veshna apex's own roof**. The Gheshar house's office claim is secondary to the Temple Line's true-line claim — but the matha's institutional interest is precisely to **combine** the two (§6.3).

**Assessment:** The Gheshar house is the matha's **preferred partner**, but not the matha's preferred king. The Gheshar house alone cannot meet all four requirements — it lacks the true-line legitimacy. However, the Gheshar-Temple Line combination (a Gheshar king backed by the Temple Line's blessing) would meet all four requirements simultaneously. This is the **resolution mechanism** (§6.3).

### 3.4 The Threnath house (the defender claim)

- **Legitimacy (requirement #1):** The weakest. The Threnath house descends from the founding garrison commander (DYN-03 §1) — a military office, not a royal one. The defender claim is respected (the Sareth wall is the basin's western shield, GEO-01 §3), but it does not rise to the level of dynastic legitimacy. The Threnath house is a **great house**, not a royal house.
- **Territorial base (requirement #2):** Strong. The Sareth holds the passes (the tolls), the foothills/forests (the iron/timber), and the Sareth mine (the silver, GEO-01 §5 — the only internal silver source). The Sareth's resources make it the **key balancing power** (DYN-04 §4) — every polity needs the Sareth's iron/timber/silver, and the Sareth can tip the balance between the rump and the Tarn.
- **Military capacity (requirement #3):** The strongest of the four. The Sareth's iron/timber funds a high-quality army (the weapons, the ships, the canal maintenance). The Sareth's geography (the wall + the passes, GEO-01 §3) is the most defensible in the basin. The Threnath house has the military capacity to **hold** the protector-of-dharma title — but not necessarily to **conquer** it from the rump and the Tarn simultaneously.
- **Matha's institutional interest (requirement #4):** Neutral to negative. The Sareth is not a matha rival (the Sareth Dhresh is a secular office, not a religious one), but the Threnath house's consecration would elevate a **purely military house** to the protector-of-dharma title — a precedent the matha has historically resisted (the founding Shreshtha was never consecrated as king; the dynasty absorbed the Shreshtha's function, DYN-01 §4). The matha's consecration of the Threnath house would effectively subordinate the matha to the military, not the reverse.

**Assessment:** The Threnath house has the strongest military capacity and the weakest legitimacy. The Threnath house can **hold** the basin but cannot be **consecrated** without a legitimacy bridge. The Threnath house's most viable path is a **Gheshar-Threnath alliance** (§6.4) — the Gheshar provides the legitimacy, the Threnath provides the military guarantee — but this requires the Threnath to subordinate itself to the Gheshar claim, which the Threnath house's pride makes difficult.

## 4. The power distribution and the wind law's agency (the structural constraints)

**The power distribution at the Dhaneshra Period's equilibrium (post-KE ~900):**

| Polity | Military | Fiscal | Legitimacy | Matha alignment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The rump (Gheshar house)** | Weak (Khor/Sareth dependent) | Moderate (bār core) | High (Great Temple + Temple Line) | Strong (Veshna apex wing) |
| **The Tarn Confederation (Keshath house)** | Strong (paddy wealth) | High (paddy plain) | Moderate (marriage precedent) | Rival (Tarn wing) |
| **The Sareth Dhaneshra (Threnath house)** | Strongest (iron/timber/silver) | High (pass tolls + mine) | Low (defender) | Neutral |
| **The Vethra** | Strong (pepper + coast) | High (pepper monopoly) | Claimed (Veshna variant) | Concordat (Kesveth) |
| **The Phre enclaves** | External (sea-lanes + silver) | High (trade + silver) | None (external) | Concordat (Voren) |

**The wind law's structural agency (GEO-03 §2/§6):**

The wind law is the world's foundational law — the seasonal wind (the wet/dry cycle) determines the agricultural calendar, the military campaigning season, and the fiscal revenue. A **great failure** (a season in which the wind fails — the grain fails — the tribute fails — the army cannot be funded) reshapes the power distribution.

The cluster phase (the deep engine, TIM-06 §4) produces great failures ~80–120 years apart. The established great failures in the Kesra Era include KE 455–460 (the Great Famine), KE 355/362/398, and the Long Night KE −265–−250. The settled phase (from ~KE −1200) produces fewer failures, but the ~700-year quiet stretch (~KE −250 → KE 455) is an anomaly — the current regime is **settled-phase** (the cluster phase has not produced a great failure since KE 455–460).

**The wind law's implication for the succession:** The wind law can **reset** the Dhaneshra Period's equilibrium — a great failure would devastate the polity whose fiscal base is most wind-dependent. The historical pattern shows that great failures disproportionately hit the **core** (the bār/pell — the irrigated, wind-dependent agriculture) and **strengthen the periphery** (the highlands/steppe — the wind-independent pastoral/agricultural systems). In the Dhaneshra Period, this means:

- A great failure hits the **rump** hardest (the bār core is the rump's fiscal base).
- A great failure hits the **Tarn** moderately (the paddy plain is wind-dependent but the Tarn's internal arrangement [the Tarnesh fourth] distributes the loss).
- A great failure **strengthens the Khor** (the steppe is wind-independent; the Khor supplies horses to the rump regardless of wind).
- A great failure **strengthens the Threnath house** (the Sareth's iron/timber/silver is wind-independent; the Threnath house's military advantage grows as the rump's fiscal base contracts).

**The wind law as a mechanism for the succession resolution:** The wind law does not determine **which** claimant is consecrated — it reshapes the **power distribution** in ways that either enable or block each claim. A great failure in the Tarn would weaken the Keshath claim; a great failure in the rump would weaken the Gheshar claim; a great failure in the Sareth would weaken the Threnath claim. The succession resolution is most likely to occur in a **stable-wind period** — when the power distribution is most predictable and the matha can assess which claimant meets the four requirements.

**The Phre's structural role:** The Phre is not a claimant — the Phre has no Veshna claim and no interest in the protector-of-dharma title. But the Phre's **silver leash** (ECO-02 §6) is the basin's monetary constraint. A Phre silver tightening (as in the Phre Silver crisis, DYN-04 §14 — KE ~800–850) weakens the rump's mints (the Kesra + Besra mints, which need silver) and strengthens the Sareth's independent silver (the Sareth mine, GEO-01 §5). The Phre's economic leverage can shift the power distribution toward the Sareth without a military action — making the Threnath house the **beneficiary of Phre economic pressure** on the rump.

## 5. The Khor's structural role (the strategic dependency)

The Khor (the four horse-clans, the seasonal assembly, FOR-01 §3) is the basin's **strategic wild card**:

- **The rump's dependency:** The rump's Khor frontier (Keshkhor, the Khor horse trade, DYN-03 §5) is the rump's source of cavalry horses (MIL-02 §2 — the Khor cavalry, the Khor-dependent striking force). Without the Khor horses, the rump's military capacity collapses.
- **The Khor's mobility:** The Khor clans practice pastoral mobility — they move with the seasons, trading horses for the rump's grain/silver and the Sareth's iron. The Khor is **not** a settled polity (NS-01 §2 — the Khor never settles). The Khor's loyalty is transactional, not dynastic.
- **The Khor's leverage:** The Khor can shift alignment between the rump and the Sareth (both need the horses). The Khor's seasonal assembly (the Kheshur as the traditional senior clan, DYN-03 §6) is the Khor's decision-making body. A Khor shift toward the Sareth would give the Threnath house the horses it needs to project power beyond the passes — a Gheshar-Threnath military alliance would become viable.

**The Khor's implication for the succession:** The Khor cannot be consecrated (the Khor is not a Veshna polity, FOR-01 §3 — the Khor faith, REL-02 §6). But the Khor's alignment **determines** which military coalition can form. The succession resolution requires a **military majority** (the claimant must hold enough of the Oren heartland to fund the temple economy, requirement #2). The Khor's shift toward the Sareth would make the **Threnath-backed Gheshar claim** militarily viable (§6.4). The Khor's loyalty to the rump would preserve the **Gheshar-only claim** (§6.3).

## 6. The resolution mechanisms (how the Empty Throne gets filled)

The four claims are evaluated against the consecration requirements (§2). The most viable resolution must satisfy **all four requirements simultaneously**: legitimacy (#1), territorial base (#2), military capacity (#3), and the matha's institutional interest (#4). Three resolution mechanisms are derivable from the established canonical structure:

### 6.1 The Keshath resolution (the Tarn route) — the least viable

**The mechanism:** The Keshath house conquers enough of the Oren heartland (the bār core) to fund the temple economy, then presses the matha to consecrate. The Tarn's paddy wealth funds the army; the Oren crossings control the trade; the Keshath claim is pressed by military force.

**Why it fails:** The Tarn is landlocked. The Tarn's army can take the bār (the Oren heartland is not heavily defended at the Tarn border), but holding it requires a **supply line through the Oren crossings** — which the Tarn already holds, but which the **Sareth controls** (the Sareth's iron/timber is the weapons supply). The Sareth's interest is to prevent the Tarn from unifying the basin (a Tarn-rump combination would dwarf the Sareth). The **Sareth would ally with the rump** against the Tarn — the iron/timber cutoff would starve the Tarn's occupation force. The matha's apex wing would **excommunicate** the Keshath house (the Second Consecration Question precedent, DYN-04 §14 — the Keshath claim recorded but not recognized). The Tarn route fails at **requirement #4** (the matha will not consecrate a Keshath king who strengthens the Tarn wing at the Veshna apex's expense) and at **requirement #3** (the Tarn cannot hold the bār against a Sareth-rump alliance without the iron supply it cannot control).

**The wind law's intervention:** A great failure in the Tarn would devastate the Keshath's fiscal base (the paddy plain is wind-dependent). A great failure in the rump would weaken the rump's defenses but not help the Tarn hold the bār.

**Conclusion:** The Keshath resolution is **causally blocked** by the Sareth's balancing power and the matha's institutional interest. It is the least viable of the four mechanisms.

### 6.2 The Threnath resolution (the Sareth route) — militarily viable, politically blocked

**The mechanism:** The Threnath house uses its iron/timber/silver advantage to build a military force that can take the Oren heartland, then presses the matha to consecrate. The Sareth's resources are wind-independent — the Threnath house can project power regardless of the wind. The Phre's silver leash (tightening the rump's mints) weakens the rump without a military action.

**Why it is viable:** The Threnath house has the **strongest military capacity** of the four. The Sareth's iron/timber/silver is wind-independent and irreplaceable — every polity needs it. The Sareth's geography (the wall + the passes) is the most defensible. The Threnath house can **hold** the basin if it takes it. The Phre's economic leverage (§4) can weaken the rump before the military action.

**Why it is blocked:** The Threnath house has the **weakest legitimacy**. The matha will not consecrate a purely military house as protector-of-dharma without a legitimacy bridge. The Threnath house's claim is **not rejected** — it is **incomplete** (the defender claim is respected, but the protector-of-dharma title requires the dynastic or marriage legitimacy the Threnath house lacks). The Threnath house would need to **share the throne** with a legitimacy source — either the Temple Line or the Gheshar house.

**The wind law's intervention:** The settled-phase wind regime (the current regime, GEO-03 §2 — the cluster phase has not produced a great failure since KE 455–460) preserves the **status quo** — the Threnath house's advantage grows slowly as the rump's fiscal base contracts. A great failure in the rump would dramatically accelerate the Threnath house's advantage (the rump's bār-dependent mints collapse; the Threnath house's iron/silver remains strong). The Threnath resolution is most likely to become viable **after** a great failure in the rump.

**Conclusion:** The Threnath resolution is **causally viable** in a wind-failure scenario or a sustained Phre silver tightening — but it requires a legitimacy bridge (the Threnath house cannot be consecrated alone). It is the **most likely military outcome** but requires political accommodation.

### 6.3 The Gheshar-Temple Line resolution (the rump route) — the most viable

**The mechanism:** The Gheshar house adopts the Temple Line as the consecration candidate — a **Temple Line restoration** with the Gheshar house as the power behind the throne. The matha consecrates the Temple Line (the true-line legitimacy, requirement #1) while the Gheshar house holds the territorial base and military capacity (requirements #2 and #3). The Gheshar house controls the Chiefs' Council, the Oren canal, Besra, and the Great Temple. The Temple Line provides the legitimacy; the Gheshar house provides the power.

**Why it is most viable:** It satisfies **all four requirements simultaneously**:
1. **Legitimacy:** The Temple Line's true-line claim is the strongest of the four (the consecrated royal house, KE 0–675). The matha has sheltered the Temple Line for generations — consecrating the Temple Line is the matha's **sacred obligation fulfilled**, not a new gamble.
2. **Territorial base:** The rump holds the bār core, the Oren canal, Besra, and Keshkhor — sufficient to fund the temple economy.
3. **Military capacity:** The rump's military is bounded by the 0.3% ceiling but is sustained by the Khor horse trade (the Khor horses, MIL-02 §2) and the Sareth iron (the weapons supply). The rump's military is **dependent** but **functional**.
4. **Matha's institutional interest:** The matha's apex wing shelters the Temple Line at the Great Temple. Consecrating the Temple Line under the Gheshar house's protection elevates the Veshna apex's authority (the Great Temple's candidate wins), does **not** elevate the Tarn wing (the Keshath claim is bypassed), and **restores the protector-of-dharma order** under the matha's preferred roof. The apex-neutrality faction's concern (the matha must not pick a side) is satisfied by the Temple Line's consecration — the Temple Line is **not** a political actor (it has no army, no administration); it is a sacred symbol. The Gheshar house provides the power without the legitimacy; the Temple Line provides the legitimacy without the power. The matha consecrates the Temple Line; the Gheshar house rules through the Temple Line.

**The structural constraint:** The Gheshar-Temple Line resolution requires the **Khor's loyalty** (the horses) and the **Sareth's neutrality** (the iron). If the Khor shifts toward the Sareth, the rump loses its cavalry; if the Sareth actively opposes the rump, the rump loses its weapons supply. The resolution is viable only if the **Sareth remains neutral or allied** — which requires the Threnath house to **accept a subordinate role** (the defender, not the king).

**The matha's internal balance:** The Tarn wing will oppose the Gheshar-Temple Line resolution (it bypasses the Keshath claim and strengthens the Veshna apex). The Veshna apex wing will support it (the Great Temple's candidate wins). The matha will split — but the **Conciliar faction** (DYN-04 §10 — the argument for the Veshna Sabha) will decide. The Sabha's 8 seats (3 rump, 1 Sareth, 1 Tarn, 1 Vethra, 1 Phre zone) give the rump a plurality (3/8), but the Tarn (1) + the Vethra (1) + the Phre zone (1) can block a majority (3/8 vs. 3/8 — a tie goes to the apex). The Veshna apex wing's hosting privilege (DYN-04 §13) gives the guru the tie-breaking vote. The matha will support the Gheshar-Temple Line resolution **if** the rump holds the territorial and military requirements.

**The wind law's intervention:** A great failure in the rump (the bār core) would devastate the Gheshar house's fiscal base and make the resolution **impossible** (requirement #2 fails). A great failure in the Tarn (the paddy plain) would **strengthen** the rump's relative position (the Tarn weakens; the rump survives). The settled-phase wind regime (the current regime) **preserves the rump's viability** — the bār core's irrigation (the Oren canal, GEO-01 §4) buffers the wind failures better than the Tarn's paddy.

**Conclusion:** The Gheshar-Temple Line resolution is **the most viable mechanism** because it satisfies all four requirements simultaneously, aligns with the matha's institutional interest, and is **structurally enabled** by the settled-phase wind regime. It is the **default resolution** — the one the established canonical structure points toward.

### 6.4 The Gheshar-Threnath coalition (the military compromise) — the fallback

**The mechanism:** The Threnath house and the Gheshar house form a **military coalition** — the Threnath house provides the military guarantee (the iron/timber/silver + the horses from a Khor-Threnath alliance), the Gheshar house provides the legitimacy (the Temple Line's blessing + the Great Temple). The coalition takes the Oren heartland from the Tarn, then settles the succession question internally. The Threnath house accepts the Gheshar-Temple Line resolution in exchange for **territorial concessions** (the passes, the iron/timber, the silver mine) and **defender status** (the Threnath house is confirmed as the protector of the western approaches, a hereditary office confirmed by the matha).

**Why it is viable:** The Sareth-rump coalition is **more powerful than the Tarn** (the Sareth's iron/timber + the rump's bār grain + the Khor horses, if the Khor shifts) outweighs the Tarn's paddy wealth. The coalition is viable if the Khor shifts alignment (the Khor Threnath alliance, §5) or if the Sareth's iron advantage alone is sufficient.

**Why it is a fallback:** The Gheshar-Temple Line resolution (§6.3) is simpler (no coalition required). The coalition requires the Threnath house to **subordinate itself** to the Gheshar claim — which the Threnath house's pride makes difficult. The Threnath house would prefer the **Threnath resolution** (§6.2) — but if the matha will not consecrate the Threnath house alone, the coalition is the Threnath house's best available option.

**The wind law's intervention:** A great failure in the rump would make the coalition **necessary** (the rump cannot hold the bār alone; the Threnath house's iron/silver is the fallback). A great failure in the Tarn would **enable** the coalition (the Tarn weakens; the coalition takes the crossings and the paddy).

**Conclusion:** The Gheshar-Threnath coalition is the **fallback mechanism** — viable when the Gheshar-Temple Line resolution is militarily challenged by the Tarn, or when the Khor shifts toward the Sareth. It is the **second most viable** mechanism.

## 7. The resolution sequence (the causal order of likelihood)

The resolution mechanisms are ranked by **causal viability** — the probability derived from the established canonical structure:

The established canonical structure — the rump's control of the Great Temple and the Temple Line, the matha's apex wing, the settled-phase wind regime, the Sareth's balancing power, the Khor's transactional loyalty — **points toward** the Gheshar house restoring the Temple Line as the consecrated king, with the Gheshar house holding the real power. This is the **resolution the established canonical structure most naturally produces**.

**The fallback (rank 2):** If the Tarn's military challenge (the Keshath route, blocked at rank 4) forces the rump to seek military alliance, the **Gheshar-Threnath coalition** becomes the resolution. The Threnath house trades military support for territorial concessions and defender status. The matha consecrates the Temple Line under the coalition's protection.

The Threnath house becomes the **only** militarily viable claimant. The matha faces the choice: consecrate the Threnath house (requirement #4 fails — the matha opposes a purely military king) or refuse and watch the Threnath house rule without consecration (the matha's authority collapses). The wind-law reset is the **most disruptive** resolution — it requires the matha to **choose between its institutional interest and its survival**.

**The Tarn route (rank 4):** The Keshath house cannot conquer and hold the bār against a Sareth-rump coalition. The Tarn route is **causally blocked**.
