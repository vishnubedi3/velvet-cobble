# Sanatan Traditions — Comparative Research

Status: RESEARCH
Date: 2026-08-26
Provenance: memory-based (agent training knowledge). Vast regional/period variation — **this is a mechanism library, not a description of one religion**. Marked **[verify]** where a specific claim is load-bearing.
Scope: how the system organizes itself — theology as grammar, institutions, political relationships, internal disputes.

## 1. Theology as a system (grammar, not creed)

- **No single creed, no prophet, no central authority, no one scripture.** Authority is dispersed across the commentarial tradition, the guru–śiṣya lineage, the temple, and the local. The shared grammar: **dharma** (duty/order, *context-dependent* — by varna, by stage of life, by time and place), **karma** (moral causation across lives), **mokṣa** (liberation as the telos), and the **varna framework** as a ritual-social grammar that is *contested from within*.
- **The load-bearing institutional fact:** because dharma is context-dependent and there is no church, the system is **inherently plural and resists centralization**. There is no single body to negotiate with, to depose, or to ally with — the "state" must manage a *mosaic*, not a partner.

## 2. Institutions

1. **Varna / jati as labor-and-social organization.** *Varna* (brāhmin/kṣatriya/vaishya/shūdra) is the ritual-ideational frame; **jāti** (thousands of endogamous groups) is the *operative* unit. Each jāti is a **self-governing corporation** (its own panchayat, rules on marriage/dining/purity, internal sanctions and records) and a **unit of occupational specialization** (priest, warrior, artisan, merchant, and the many sub-castes). **Mechanism:** a decentralized division of labor + social order **without any central authority**. **Political effect:** the state manages a *mosaic of corporations*, not one community; the jāti is the substrate of the economic division of labor.
2. **Dharma-shastra legal pluralism.** The dharma-shastras (e.g., the Manusmriti and its commentators) as an "ideal" law interpreted by local brāhmans, coexisting with **customary (jāti/panchayat) law** and **royal law**. **Mechanism:** a **plural legal order** — the king's law for public order, the jāti's law for the internal, the brāhman's law for the ritual/moral — with the king invoking the dharma-shastras for legitimation.
3. **Bhakti / revival movements as anti-institutional forces.** Recurring vernacular devotional movements (the Ālvārs/Nāyaṉārs; later Kābīr, Rāmānanda, Caitanya) that challenge the brāhmanical/temple orthodoxy — direct devotion, vernacular language, varying anti-caste stances. **Mechanism:** a **recurring orthodoxy-vs-revival tension** (temple/brāhman/ritual vs. vernacular/direct/anti-hierarchical) that is *not* a one-off reformation but a standing dynamic. **Political effect:** the revival creates a **vernacular literary culture** and a **popular devotional identity that cuts across varna** — a threat to the orthodoxy and simultaneously a *tool for the state* (a popular devotion is easier to patronize than a brāhmanical monopoly).
4. **The temple as an economic institution.** The temple as **landowner** (devanama grants), **employer** (priests, artisans, dancers, musicians), **market** (temple fairs = trade), **repository** (granaries, workshops), and **center of local administration**. **Mechanism:** a fiscal sink + a local economic engine + a legitimation anchor. (Pairs directly with the Vijayanagara register.)
5. **The guru–śiṣya and the matha.** The guru lineage (the monastic-educational units, e.g., the later Śaṅkara mathas) as a **mobile institutional node** that projects influence across regions **without a territorial state**.

## 3. Relationship to politics

- **No church–state relationship** — instead: the **king as protector/patron of dharma** (the *dharmarāja* ideal, contested), the **brāhman as legitimator** (consecration, ritual), in a **symbiosis with friction** ("the king needs the brāhman's ritual; the brāhman needs the king's protection"). The state **manages the jāti mosaic**: grants land to jātis, sets boundaries, adjudicates *between* them, but (usually) does not control their internal law.

## 4. Internal disputes

- Varna (the ideal frame) vs. jāti (the actual diversity). The **orthodoxy-vs-revival** standing dynamic. **Regional divergence** (the Dravidian temple culture vs. the Vedic/āśramic north; the bhakti's north–south split). Brāhmanical sub-caste and school disputes.

## 5. Mechanisms available for Samur transformation

- **Varna/jāti as a decentralized division of labor + social order** (the substrate of the economic division of labor without central planning; the state as manager of a corporate mosaic).
- **Dharma-shastra legal pluralism** (king's law + corporate law + priestly law coexisting).
- **The recurring orthodoxy-vs-revival dynamic** (a tool the state can use to manage a popular religious identity against an entrenched orthodoxy).
- **The temple as an economic institution** (fiscal sink + local engine + legitimation anchor).
- **The guru/matha as a mobile institutional node** (influence without territory).
- **The king as protector of dharma** (legitimation *without* theocracy).
- **The absence of a single negotiable religious body** (the state must manage a mosaic, not a partner) — and its effect on state–religion strategy.
