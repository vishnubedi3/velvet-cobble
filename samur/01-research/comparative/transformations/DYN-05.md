### Post-fragmentation succession disputes (comparative)

**Analytical reference 1: The post-fragmentation succession as a legitimacy-military compromise**

The pattern: in post-fragmentation polities, the claimant with the strongest legitimacy rarely has the strongest military, and vice versa. The resolution is typically a **legitimacy-military compromise** — a coalition between the legitimate claimant and the militarily dominant power, with the legitimizing figure as the crowned king and the military dominant power as the real ruler.

- **The Eastern Roman post-crisis pattern:** The legitimacy claimant (the true line, the Nikephoros succession) + the military dominant house (the Anatolian strategos) → the crowned emperor + the real power in the military house. The resolution stabilizes the state by satisfying both legitimacy and military requirements.
- **The post-Mughal succession pattern:** Multiple claimants with competing legitimacy bases (the true line, the governor's house, the military strongman) → the resolution is typically a coalition (the legitimate claimant crowned; the military strongman as the real power) or a military imposition (the strongman takes both legitimacy and power, paying a legitimacy cost).

**Samur version (DYN-05 §6.3):** The Gheshar-Temple Line resolution — the Temple Line provides the legitimacy (the true line, the consecrated royal house, KE 0–675); the Gheshar house provides the power (the Shreshtha office, the Chiefs' Council, the bār core). The crowned Temple Line king + the real-power Gheshar house. This is the **legitimacy-military compromise pattern** applied to the Samur's specific structure.

### Post-fragmentation institutional evolution (comparative)

**Analytical reference 2: The institution's adaptation to fragmentation**

The pattern: a central institution (the temple, the church, the court) that survives fragmentation must evolve a **conciliar or concordat mechanism** to remain coherent across multiple polities. The institution's apex (the patriarch, the pope, the guru) must share authority with regional heads to maintain unity.

- **The post-1054 Eastern Orthodox pattern:** The patriarchate of Constantinople + the autocephalous churches → the conciliar mechanism (the councils) to maintain unity across political boundaries. The resolution: a **conciliar institution with apex primacy** — the patriarch retains nominal primacy, but the autocephalous churches govern themselves.
- **The post-Carolingian church pattern:** The papal authority + the regional episcopates → the Concordats (the medieval concordats between the papacy and the secular rulers) to define the church's autonomy and the ruler's authority. The resolution: separate agreements with each polity, preserving the church's spiritual authority while acknowledging political reality.

**Samur version (DYN-05 §6.3/§8.1):** The Veshna matha's adaptation — the geographic fragmentation (the 7 Head Temples in 5 polities) forces the **conciliar + concordat** system (the Veshna Sabha + the five Concordats, DYN-04 §13). The Gheshar-Temple Line resolution **restores** the apex institution's kingmaker function (the matha consecrates the Temple Line) while preserving the concordat system (the five Concordats remain). The resolution does not dissolve the matha's conciliar adaptation; it adds the consecration function on top of it.

### Wind law as structural agency (comparative)

**Analytical reference 3: Environmental stress as a political reset mechanism**

The pattern: in agrarian empires, a significant environmental shock (a famine, a flood, a failed monsoon) disproportionately weakens the political center (the core's tax base collapses) and relatively strengthens the periphery (the steppe, the highlands, the trading networks). The environmental shock **resets** the political equilibrium, enabling a military claimant who was previously blocked by institutional constraints.

- **The late Ming pattern:** The transition crisis (the 1640s famine, the northern frontier failure) → the Ming center collapses; the peripheral claimants (the rebel, the Manchu) become viable. The environmental shock enables the military resolution that institutional constraints had previously blocked.
- **The Late Bronze Age collapse pattern:** The systemic disruption (the ~1200 BCE crisis) → the palace economies collapse; the peripheral peoples (the Sea Peoples, the Aramaeans) fill the vacuum. The environmental/disease shock resets the political map.

**Samur version (DYN-05 §4/§6.3/§7):** The wind law's great failures reset the Dhaneshra Period's equilibrium. A great failure in the rump (the bār core) would devastate the Gheshar house's fiscal base and block the Gheshar-Temple Line resolution. A great failure in the Tarn would enable it. The wind law is the **structural reset mechanism** — the Threnath resolution (rank 3) becomes viable **only after** a wind-law reset. The settled-phase wind regime (the current regime, GEO-03 §2) **preserves** the default resolution (the Gheshar-Temple Line). This is the **environmental stress → political reset pattern** applied to the Samur's specific wind law.

### The empty throne as a mechanism (comparative)

**Analytical reference 4: The vacant throne as a political equilibrium**

The pattern: in polities with a consecration or coronation mechanism, a **vacant throne** can become a stable political equilibrium if the cost of filling it (the war among claimants) exceeds the benefit (the consecration's legitimacy). The vacant throne is preserved by the **balance of power** — no claimant is strong enough to fill it without war, and all claimants prefer the vacant throne to the war.

- **The Holy Roman Empire interregnum pattern (1254–1273):** The contested succession → the interregnum (the vacant throne) → the balance of power between the great houses → the pragmatic resolution (the election of a compromise candidate after 19 years). The vacant throne is preserved by the balance of power, resolved by a compromise candidate.
- **The Polish-Lithuanian elective monarchy pattern:** The liberum veto → the elective throne → the foreign influence (the foreign king candidates, the noble factions) → the instability. The vacant throne is a **feature**, not a bug — it prevents the consolidation of power that would threaten the nobles.

**Samur version (DYN-05 §2/§6.3):** The Consecration Moratorium (the matha will not consecrate while the claims are contested) is a **stable political equilibrium** — the cost of filling the Empty Throne (the war among claimants) exceeds the benefit (the consecration's legitimacy). The equilibrium holds as long as the power distribution remains balanced. The Gheshar-Temple Line resolution **ends** the equilibrium by satisfying all four consecration requirements simultaneously — the matha's institutional interest shifts from preserving the moratorium to filling it before the claimant becomes strong enough to不需要 it. This is the **vacant throne as equilibrium → resolution when requirements are met** pattern.
