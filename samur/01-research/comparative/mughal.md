# Mughal Empire (1526–1857) — Comparative Research

Status: RESEARCH
Date: 2026-08-26
Provenance: memory-based (agent training knowledge). Specific figures/dates marked **[verify]** before load-bearing use.
Scope: material conditions, institutions, mechanisms, and decline — as a model for a composite agrarian-imperial state.

## 1. Material foundation

- **Heartland:** the Gangetic plain (the subcontinent's most fertile alluvial agricultural zone) plus Punjab; frontiers on the Afghan plateau (west), the Deccan (south), and Bengal (east).
- **Population:** North India estimated ~60–100 million by the 17th c. **[verify range]**; at its peak the Mughal state covered roughly a third of the subcontinent's population.
- **Revenue base:** agriculture (wheat, rice, sugarcane, indigo, cotton), plus long-distance trade (Bengal muslin, Deccan horses, salt) and river transport on the Ganges as the cheap logistics backbone.
- **Strategic setting:** a large interior agricultural empire ringed by mountain/plateau frontiers (Safavid Iran, Uzbek Transoxiana, the Deccan sultanates, the hills of the Sikh/Gujarati west) — a permanent multi-front condition.

## 2. Core institutions and mechanisms

1. **Mansabdari (rank-and-pay office).** All officers — including hereditary nobles — held a *mansab* (a rank with a fixed personal retinue and salary), paid from a *jagir* (a land assignment that moved with the office, not the man). **Mechanism:** converts a hereditary aristocracy into a salaried imperial corps; personal service to the emperor becomes the price of income; rotation of jagirs is meant to prevent territorial entrenchment. **Unintended:** elite wealth becomes dependent on continuous imperial favor, so the **court becomes the only source of wealth** — court faction, intrigue, and execution become the dominant politics. When central authority weakened, jagirs became de facto hereditary, and provinces (Bengal, Awadh, Rohilkhand) regionalized.
2. **Zabt revenue assessment.** Periodic land measurement/assessment (systematized under Akbar with Todar Mal), payment in cash where possible, plus state monopoly over key trades (horses, textiles, salt, betel) and transit/market dues. **Mechanism:** a cash-revenue state; the monetization demand forces market integration and makes the state the largest employer and purchaser. **Unintended:** the push for cash draws smallholders into cash crops (indigo, cotton, sugar) and into moneylender debt; when later emperors inflated revenue targets, it produced peasant and artisan revolts (Satnami 1672, the Jats, the Pabhis under Aurangzeb).
3. **Religious policy as elite management.** Akbar's accommodation (jizya remitted, the Ibadat Khana, the court culture of the Din-i-Ilahi) → Jahangir/Shah Jahan drift toward the orthodox-Islamic nobility → Aurangzeb's revival (jizya reimposed, temple destruction). **Mechanism:** religious policy is a **coalition-management tool** to buy the alliance of one elite bloc, not primarily theology. **Unintended:** each reversal manufactures a new grievance class; the Deccan campaigns drained resources while the religious policy alienated the non-Muslim majority who supplied most of the revenue.
4. **The imperial court as the state.** The palace is the administrative and fiscal center (the diwan, the wazir, the patronage networks, the imperial harem). **Mechanism:** "where the emperor is, the state is"; wealth and decision concentrate in the capital. **Unintended:** the court's scale makes it a single point of failure — a weak or absent emperor paralyzes the whole system.
5. **Military and war finance.** A gunpowder army of mansabs with fixed quotas of cavalry, growing artillery and foot; financed by revenue surplus, state trade profit, and imperial borrowing from bankers (e.g., the Surat moneylenders). **Mechanism:** the army is an extension of the fiscal-mansab system — soldiers are paid officials.

## 3. Elite structure and factions

- The Turco-Persian *mansabdar* nobility (the core of the salaried corps); the Rajput and later Deccani/Hindu *mansabdars* (co-opted hereditary warriors); the *ulama* and Sufi orders (legitimation and urban social infrastructure); the merchant-banker class (the financiers of war). The recurring fault line is **court faction vs. regional/jagirdar interest**, and **orthodox vs. accommodative religious policy** as the instrument that rearranges the factions.

## 4. Expansion, contraction, and decline

- **Aurangzeb's Deccan overstretch (~1680s–1707):** conquest without integration capacity — new provinces produced revenue that could not be absorbed by the mansab/jagir system, so the *cost of holding* exceeded the *revenue of holding*. **Root causes of decline:** the **jagirdari crisis** (arable land insufficient to assign to the mansab corps); inflation of revenue targets; Deccan overstretch; court factionalization; frontier pressure. **Trigger:** Aurangzeb's death (1707) and the succession war, which shattered the jagir system.
- **Fragmentation as composite monarchy:** the successor states (Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad, the Marathas) each inherited the Mughal institutional toolkit (mansab-like office, cash revenue, court culture) but rooted it in a regional elite base. The empire did not so much vanish as **distribute its institutions among its provinces**.

## 5. Mechanisms available for Samur transformation

- **Ranked, salaried office vs. hereditary tenure** — and its failure mode when there is not enough land to pay all the offices (the "jagirdari crisis").
- **Cash-revenue monetization chain** — cash demand → cash crops → moneylender → peasant debt → revolt when targets inflate.
- **Religion as an elite-coalition tool** with grievance-class accumulation across policy reversals.
- **The court as the state's center** and its single-point-of-failure property.
- **Conquest without integration capacity** as a fiscal-military overstretch death-spiral.
- **Composite-monarchy fragmentation** — the post-imperial pattern where successor states inherit the toolkit but regionalize its elite base.
