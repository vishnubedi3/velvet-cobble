# Christianity — Comparative Research

Status: RESEARCH
Date: 2026-08-26
Provenance: memory-based (agent training knowledge). Marked **[verify]** where a specific claim is load-bearing.
Scope: the system as a **universal, territorial, hierarchical institution** — the papacy, the monastic order, the canon law, the schism, and the just-war/crusade.

## 1. Theology as a system

- The core: the **Trinity**, the **Incarnation** (the God-man), the **sacraments** (the seven, as institutionalized grace), and the **scripture** (the Bible/canon). **The load-bearing institutional fact:** the **church as a universal institutional hierarchy** — the **bishop, the priest, the deacon, the pope/patriarch** — a **visible, territorial, hierarchical institution** (in contrast to Judaism's portability and Islam's madhhab-pluralism). The church is a **"state within a state"**: it has its own **canon law, its own jurisdiction, its own fiscal base (the tithes), and its own court**.

## 2. Institutions

1. **The papal–imperial dualism and the investiture.** The **papacy** (the Roman bishop as universal spiritual authority) vs. the **emperor** (the political authority); the **investiture controversy** (11th–12th c. — "the emperor appoints the bishop" vs. "the pope appoints the bishop"); the **"two swords"** (the spiritual vs. the temporal power). **Mechanism:** a **structural dualism** — two powers, each claiming a supremacy, that must interact; the **bishop's appointment is both a spiritual and a political act**, so it is the flashpoint. **Political effect:** the "two swords" as a **permanent structural tension**; the emperor needs the pope's coronation (legitimation), the pope needs the emperor's protection (military); the poles are **Caesaropapism** (the state controls the church — the Byzantine model) vs. **papal supremacy** (the church controls the state — the Roman model).
2. **The monastic order as an institutional innovator.** The **monastery** (the Benedictine, the Cistercian; later the Franciscan/Dominican) as a **self-governing economic-educational-spiritual unit** (*ora et labora*); the monastery as **landowner, producer, school, and hospital**. **Mechanism:** a **parallel institutional base** (the monastic land, the monastic economy) outside the secular state's direct control; the **order as a mobile institutional node** (the abbey, the priory, the cell) projecting influence across regions; the **mendicant** (Franciscan/Dominican) as the **urban** institution (the friary in the city, vs. the rural abbey). **Political effect:** the order as a **power broker** (the abbot as a local lord; the order as a landowner); the **mendicant as the papal instrument** (preaching, the inquisition, urban penetration).
3. **The canon law as a parallel legal system.** The **Corpus Iuris Canonici** (from Gratian on) as the church's legal system; the **canonical court** (marriage, the testament, the oath, the clerical crime); the **appeal to Rome** (the Roman curia as the highest court). **Mechanism:** a **parallel legal system that overlaps with the secular** (marriage, the testament, the oath) — creating **jurisdictional competition** (the "benefit of clergy," the "appeal") **and a central legal authority** (the curia).
4. **The schism as a political weapon.** The **East–West schism (1054)** (Orthodox vs. Catholic); the **Western schism (1378–1417)** (two/three popes); the **conciliarism** (the council vs. the pope). **Mechanism:** the schism as a product of the **political** (East–West = the Byzantine vs. the Roman political division; the Western schism = the French vs. the Roman papal faction); the **rival leader as a political weapon** (the "antipope").
5. **The crusade / just-war theology as an imperial tool.** The **crusade** (11th–13th c.) as the **institutionalized holy war**; the **"just war"** (Augustine, Aquinas) as the theological frame. **Mechanism:** the crusade as the **institutionalized mobilization of the European nobility** (the "holy war" as the political-military instrument, redirecting the violence) + the "just war" as the **theological legitimation of secular violence**.

## 3. Relationship to politics

- The **church–state relationship:** the **"two swords"** (the dualism); **Caesaropapism** (the state controls the church — the Byzantine model) vs. **papal supremacy** (the church controls the state — the Roman model); the **concordat** (the negotiated relationship); the **tithes** (the church's fiscal base, ~10% of the produce). The church's **sanctions** on the secular: the **interdict** (the excommunication of a territory), the **excommunication** (of an individual), the **inquisition** (internal discipline, later).

## 4. Internal disputes

- The **East–West** (the *filioque*; the primacy of Rome); the **Western schism** (the two popes); the **conciliarism** (the council vs. the pope); the **reform** movements (Wycliffe, Hus; the later Reformation — out of scope here); the **monastic** disputes (the Benedictine vs. the mendicant).
